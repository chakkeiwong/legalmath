# Paragraph 14: technical confidence, reliance and SFC satisfaction

9 October 2026. Original question `p14`; original obligations 4, 10, 21 and 42,
whose exact identities remain in mission.json. The October 6 paragraph-14 dossier
remains the baseline. Exact retained passages are in
`artifacts/legal-mission-control/2026-10-09/continuation/p14-attachment/source-excerpts.json`.

The new contextual support concerns the scope of “a high level of confidence”.
Appendix A(b)(i) uses the same wording specifically for the smart-contract audit.
It also expressly distinguishes obtaining an audit from being able to demonstrate
that reliance on it is reasonable. This supports a more precise product answer
without deciding the remaining dispute about footnote 5 and the request trigger.

## The argument and its strongest contrary

The main paragraph requires confirmation and, on request, demonstration to the
SFC's satisfaction of management/operational soundness, ownership record keeping
and smart-contract integrity. Footnote 5 names, at a minimum, paragraphs 10–13
compliance and the absence of smart-contract vulnerabilities/security flaws
“with a high level of confidence”. The earlier dossier left the reach of that
phrase as an unresolved interpretation premise.

Appendix A(b)(i), which paragraph 10's footnote refers to where applicable, is a
specific contextual parallel. It says that the smart-contract audit should focus
on reviewing whether the smart contract is not subject to vulnerabilities or
security flaws with a high level of confidence. That sentence has an explicit
technical-audit subject and the same technical object and confidence language as
footnote 5. It therefore supports reading the confidence qualification as directed
to the technical assurance about the smart contract, rather than automatically
turning it into a numerical confidence threshold for every paragraph 10–13 duty.

This is contextual evidence, not proof of exclusive attachment. Footnote 5 has its
own coordinated confirmation/demonstration sentence. An interpretation might
still give its final qualification a wider evidential reach, and the Appendix is
a non-exhaustive, applicable-context reference rather than an express priority
rule. The product must retain that contrary. Nothing inspected establishes a
percentage, a statistical coverage guarantee, or that technical confidence is
identical to the SFC's satisfaction.

The same Appendix note expressly says that an intermediary relying on a third-
party smart-contract audit should be able to demonstrate that it is reasonable
to rely on that audit. The fact that a report exists and says “high confidence”
does not establish the basis for reasonable reliance, the scope of its review,
its application to the deployed contract, or the SFC's satisfaction. Applying
the Appendix to a particular provider remains qualified by the circular's
“where applicable” language and the actual role/arrangement. It is not a universal
requirement that every paragraph-14 answer be supported by a third-party audit.

## Distinguishing examples and answer constraints

Suppose an applicable paragraph-14 request has been made. A report expresses high
confidence about contract version V1, but the arrangement under assessment uses
V2 with a material unreviewed change. The report's existence does not supply the
missing relation to V2. This is an illustrative conditional mismatch, not a
finding that all upgrades require a new audit or that a particular report is
invalid. A supported version/scope-equivalence argument could change the result.

Even a justified technical assessment of the deployed contract does not by itself
demonstrate paragraphs 10–13 compliance, including the proper ownership-records
requirement. Conversely, evidence addressing ownership records cannot establish
the absence of smart-contract flaws. The local technical-confidence reading
preserves these different objects. A wider-confidence rival must justify its
reach and cannot merely substitute the technical report for all required content.

SFC satisfaction is a further proposition. Neither possession of an audit report
nor a provider's asserted confidence proves it. A paragraph-15 request for audit
or verification and a paragraph-16 request for a satisfactory legal opinion are
also separate from the paragraph-14 request. One actual communication may contain
several requests, but that requires inspection of its content; their identities
cannot be inferred from the existence of one report.

When no paragraph-14 demonstration request has been made, A and C continue to
condition demonstration on a request, while B relies on the footnote's unqualified
wording as an independent standing duty. The contextual audit passage does not
settle that disagreement. It does not remove the separately proposed standing
confirmation requirement, waive paragraphs 10–13, or license treating an unknown
request as an established absence. When a request is established, A/B/C may agree
on duty activation while still disagreeing about content or standards. This
result therefore adds no new trigger truth table.

## Result and remaining evidence

Before: “high confidence” had several possible scopes, with no specific source
argument for one, and an audit remained one among the separately named acts.
After: the repeated technical-audit formulation supplies a particular argument
for local smart-contract scope; the express reasonable-reliance sentence makes
an additional evidence dependency visible. The product should distinguish report
existence, justified reliance, technical content, underlying compliance and SFC
satisfaction in a requested-demonstration answer.

This is a supported interpretive argument and a concrete sufficiency challenge,
not a final choice of A/B/C. The missing decisive authority is a clarification or
applicable construction addressing whether footnote 5 changes the main text's
request condition and how the confidence phrase relates to the other objects.
An actual request, contract version, report scope and correspondence evidencing
SFC satisfaction are arrangement-specific inputs; their absence does not stop
conditional product development.
