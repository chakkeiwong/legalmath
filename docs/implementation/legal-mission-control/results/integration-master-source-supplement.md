## A material omission in the P10 research corpus

The official SFC codes index labels the current Handbook 2 October 2024; the
downloaded PDF identifies itself as the October 2024 fifth edition. Its HTTP
Last-Modified date is 7 May 2026. These are different metadata fields: the
server date does not establish a legal amendment or its commencement. The index
also supplies a previous version labelled 1 January 2019–1 October 2024. All ten
selected passages below match that previous version after whitespace
normalization. This is a passage comparison, not proof that every provision or
authorization condition remained unchanged.

The previous campaign combines selected UT Chapter 6 material and opening
pages with the circulars and judgments. It does not contain the Handbook's
Overarching Principles Section. That omission matters to the question about
standards for conduct, selection and remediation. It is our source-selection
gap, not an unknowable legal standard or a missing client fact.

Section I, clauses 1.1–1.6, explains the Handbook's authorization purpose,
applicable general and product-specific requirements and the consequences of
noncompliance. Clause 1.5 says the Handbook does not have the force of law;
clause 1.6 distinguishes evidential and regulatory consequences from automatic
liability to proceedings. Clause 1.7(j) identifies the relevant Product Provider
by product type. These scope provisions must accompany a substantive clause.
For unit trusts and mutual funds, UT 1.1–1.2 further addresses authorization,
recognised jurisdictions, material compliance and possible waivers.

Within that scope, Section I 3.8 requires the Product Provider to discharge its
functions with due skill, care and diligence. Clauses 5.4–5.5 address care and
diligence in selecting counterparties and service providers, and due enquiries
into competence and resources to perform their obligations and relevant
product-code requirements. Clause 4.1(c) calls for prompt notification of a
material Handbook breach; 4.1(d) requires best endeavours to take appropriate
remedial action promptly. Its note explains investor protection and market
integrity as the purpose of remediation. These provisions supply actual
standards relevant to a properly scoped conduct analysis.

They do not say that selecting a competent registrar proves proper records,
that diligence excuses every substantive defect, or that remediation
retroactively erases a breach. For a provider and product within the Handbook's
scope, the conditional assessment should therefore examine retained
responsibility, skill/care/diligence in the actual functions, provider selection
and enquiries, substantive record and operational requirements, and the
notification and remedial response separately. That is a better specified
assessment than merely reporting that no standard has been supplied. The
instrument-specific consequence of an incident and its assessment interval
still require analysis and facts.

UT 5.5(e) requires ongoing supervision and regular monitoring of delegates'
competence where **investment management** is delegated, while retaining the
management company's responsibilities. UT 6.8 assigns maintenance of the
register to the scheme or, for a unit trust, the trustee or its appointee.
UT 6.11C requires reasonable care, skill and diligence in selection, appointment
and monitoring of a third party engaged in **valuation**, together with ongoing
supervision and periodic review. Neither specialized provision establishes a
universal supervision frequency or a universal P10 fulfillment test.

The relevant PDF pages are 7–8 and 12–14 for the general provisions, 24 for
UT scope, 37 for UT 5.5(e), 42 for UT 6.8 and 43 for UT 6.11C. Pages 12–14 were
also inspected as images. The ten exact retained passages and edition
comparisons are in `source-supplement.json`. The supplied analysis is conditional
on the actor, product, authorization conditions, modifications and period.
