The additional conclusion is narrow but useful: **agreement between two records
does not establish that either record reflects the legally relevant ownership
interest**. The sources give a specific reason to keep those assessments separate.
This removes a possible shortcut in assessing the registrar-defect example; it
does not decide whether paragraph 10 itself imposes a conduct or outcome standard.

## The additional source argument

Paragraph 10 preserves the Product Provider's responsibility despite outsourcing.
Footnote 1 gives the importance of proper records reflecting investors' ownership
as its reason and refers to applicable Appendix Part A factors. Paragraph 11
separately says providers should ensure proper records of token holders'
ownership interests. Paragraph 17(a)-(b) requires disclosure of settlement
finality and the ownership representation, including legal/beneficial title and
interests in the product. These provisions identify a relationship between the
records and the rights they represent, rather than a requirement that two data
files merely contain identical values.

Appendix A(c)(iii) makes the distinction explicit. Its note asks, among other
things, whether ownership interests in underlying assets are legally valid under
the applicable governing laws, whether there are regular reconciliations between
records for extrinsic rights and distributed-ledger tokens, and whether any
encumbrance affects those rights. The legal validity, reconciliation and
encumbrance inquiries appear as distinct considerations. They are not presented
as interchangeable evidence or an exhaustive test. Appendix footnote 1 also
recognizes models in which the blockchain is merely a secondary record.

The resulting inference is evidential. To rely on a particular reconciliation as
support for proper ownership records, the analysis must explain what rights the
compared entries are intended to represent, why the chosen records are probative
of those rights, which holders/interests/times the comparison covers, and what it
does with discrepancies. Agreement alone leaves each of those matters open.
This is a reasoned application of the retained source language, not an additional
statutory checklist that every arrangement must implement in the same way.

## Two conditional counterexamples

Assume, solely for the first example, that the applicable terms and law establish
that holder H has 10 units in product P at the settled assessment time. The
registrar file and its on-chain mirror both show zero because both copied the
same omitted entry. A reconciliation between them reports a perfect match. That
result is consistent with both records failing to reflect H's stipulated interest.
It therefore cannot by itself establish proper records. The existence and
quantity of H's right are assumed inputs to this example; no client entitlement
has been inferred from a token label.

For the converse example, assume that the legally relevant, complete holder
register correctly records H's settled interest, the blockchain is a secondary
record, and the applicable terms specify the status and timing of updates. A
discrepancy in that secondary copy is evidence to investigate, but its existence
alone does not determine whether the required ownership records are improper.
The difference may still breach an operational requirement or the applicable
update standard. The example supplies no tolerance, grace period, materiality
threshold or excuse for a known error. It shows why the nature and role of the
record must be established before classifying a discrepancy.

These counterexamples distinguish three propositions that the next product
experiment must preserve: a reconciliation occurred; the compared records agreed;
and the relevant records accurately represent established ownership interests.
Neither of the first two entails the third. Nor does a mismatch identify which
record, if either, is legally dispositive. A supplied assumption about register
primacy must remain visible in the answer.

## Effect on the paragraph-10 alternatives

The strongest outcome argument remains paragraph 10's responsibility *for*
operational soundness and records, reinforced by “fully responsible”, the
ownership-protection rationale, and paragraph 11's substantive records requirement.
If the scenario supplies an established failure of the selected outcome standard,
the outcome reading fails even when supervision was competent.

The strongest contrary remains the distinction between continuing accountability
and separate performance requirements. Paragraph 11 can fail while a provider
still bears effective responsibility; retaining responsibility is not immunity
from a separate deficiency. Paragraphs 12 and 19 make risk management and
supervision relevant without saying that good conduct exhausts every requirement.
Likewise, the intermediary circular's due-skill/care/diligence language and its
separate responsibility-despite-outsourcing clause do not state a safe harbour
for every properly supervised defect. UT 6.8 identifies who may maintain a member
register; it does not supply a general discharge test. UT 6.11C concerns valuation
delegates and cannot set a universal tokenisation supervision cadence.

No new source in this task selects a unique paragraph-10 interpretation or proves
that remediation extinguishes an earlier deficiency. The registrar example must
therefore report paragraph-10 fulfillment separately from any independently
established paragraph-11 records failure. It must not report “supervision occurred,
therefore responsibility was fulfilled”, or silently choose paragraph 11 as the
answer to the paragraph-10 question.

## Before and after, and the next discriminating evidence

Before: the dossier required a governing ownership/propriety standard and listed
reconciliation results among observable inputs. The conditional outcome O remained
an assessed judgment. After: Appendix A(c)(iii), paragraph 17 and the secondary-
record qualification support a specific challenge to a reconciliation-based
sufficiency argument. A matching pair of records can share a rights error; a
secondary-record discrepancy has a different evidential role from an established
failure in the relevant record set. This narrows what a product answer may infer
from reconciliation without inventing an exhaustive proper-records test.

This is a limited substantive advance in the evidence argument. The main
paragraph-10 standard remains unresolved, and all four missing premises of
obligation 2 retain their original identities. It is not independent legal
adjudication or an observed correction of a live model answer.

The precise public-source question still needed is whether an applicable SFC
clarification, authorization condition or adjudicated interpretation treats a
specified records deficiency despite competent retained supervision/remediation
as failure of paragraph 10 itself, rather than only of an adjacent requirement.
The strongest useful evidence would address that contrast and the assessment
period directly. A generic outsourcing policy or a valuation standard would not
answer it. Actual terms, legally established rights, record roles, coverage and
chronology are needed only for a concrete arrangement-specific assessment.

analysis. The result challenges a sufficiency inference; it does not establish
that a particular record is wrong or a particular provider is in breach.
