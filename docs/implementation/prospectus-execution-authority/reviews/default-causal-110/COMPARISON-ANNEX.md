Let C denote cessation, D disposal, Vc/Pc the stated value/payment consequences
attributable to cessation, Vd/Pd those attributable to disposal, and Vj/Pj those
attributable to combined conduct. The current optional consumer compares:

* A = C and D and Vj and Pj.
* B = (C and Vc and Pc) or (D and Vd and Pd).
* E = C or (D and Vd and Pd).

The earlier reasoning002 proof used shared V,P in all three expressions. Under
that explicit restriction, A implies B and B implies E. In the current model,
A implies E because A entails C. B implies E: its first branch entails C and
its second is E's second branch. A does not imply B when C,D,Vj,Pj are true
and both individual chains are false. B does not imply A when C,D,Vc,Pc are
true and Vj,Pj are false. E implies neither A nor B when C alone is true.
These are Boolean implications for a nonexhaustive candidate set. The first two
counterexamples require legal and factual scrutiny of the causal definitions;
they are not proof that a particular real transaction has those properties.

The joint-only case stipulates that combined conduct causes both stated
consequences, while neither individual conduct has its own complete causal chain.
The individual-chain case stipulates cessation-related consequences and both
conducts, without combined-conduct attribution. Neither assumes an additive
financial model or a rule that joint causation follows from individual causation.
